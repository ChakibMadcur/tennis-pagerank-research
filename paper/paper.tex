\begin{abstract}
Official sports rankings follow different rules: tournament points in tennis, an Elo-type rating in international football, and league tables in team sports. Using ideas from network science, I rank competitors with weighted PageRank on the directed network of match results, where every loser points to the winner and each link is weighted by the number of wins. I compare these PageRank rankings with the official rankings over five decades of men's and women's professional tennis, and in international football, the Premier League, the NBA, the NHL and the NFL. The two rankings are well correlated, so I focus on their differences. I compare their predictability by using each ranking at the end of one season to predict the winners of the next season's matches, and I examine where the rankings disagree: how much one upset of the world number one is worth, and which players gain or lose places depending on where and in which tournaments they play. The aim is to understand what each ranking rule rewards.
\end{abstract}

\noindent\textbf{Keywords:} PageRank; network science; ranking; sports analytics; tennis; prediction.

\section{Introduction}

Every sport ranks its competitors, and every sport does it differently. The ATP and WTA rankings in tennis add up fixed points for the round a player reaches in each tournament; the FIFA men's ranking of national teams uses an Elo-type rating; leagues such as the Premier League, the NBA, the NHL and the NFL use tables of wins and points. These rankings matter: they decide who enters tournaments, who is seeded and who is called the best in the world. Yet none of them looks directly at the whole set of results: who beat whom, and how strong those opponents were. In tennis, two players who reach the same round of the same event receive the same points, whether they beat the world number one or a qualifier on the way.

Network science offers a different view. A season can be represented as a directed network in which every match is a link from the loser to the winner. In such a network a strong competitor is one who beats other strong competitors, the idea behind PageRank \citep{brin1998}. In this paper I weight each link by the number of wins and rank competitors with weighted PageRank (WPR), and I compare the result with the official rankings.

The comparison has three parts.
\begin{enumerate}
  \item \textbf{Correlation.} How well correlated are WPR and the official ranking, in every season from 1973 to 2025 in men's tennis, from 1984 in women's tennis, and in five other competitions?
  \item \textbf{Predictability.} Which ranking better predicts the future? Each ranking at the end of one season predicts the winners of the next season's matches, and I count how often each one is right.
  \item \textbf{Disagreements.} Where the two rankings differ, why? I measure how much one upset of the world number one is worth under each ranking, and which players gain or lose places depending on the tournaments they enter and the region where they play.
\end{enumerate}

\paragraph{Prior work.} Ranking tennis players with PageRank on a weighted network of results is not new. \citet{radicchi2011} built the network of all professional matches from 1968 to 2010, weighting the link from $j$ to $i$ by the number of times $j$ lost to $i$, and used the same random walk as here. \citet{dingle2013} compared PageRank with the ATP and WTA rankings and used it to predict matches; \citet{aronson2017} weighted each match by round, surface and age; \citet{michieli2018} and \citet{needham2018} also ranked players with win-weighted PageRank; and \citet{asztalos2026} recently compared network measures with official scores in tennis and fencing. Outside tennis, links have been weighted by the score difference in American football \citep{govan2008} and by wins and goals in international football \citep{lazova2015}. The name ``weighted PageRank'' itself comes from web search \citep{xing2004}. I therefore do not claim to be the first to use weighted PageRank in sports. What this paper adds is a systematic comparison with the official rankings over five decades of men's and women's tennis and across sports whose official rankings follow different rules, a test of predictability on every following season, and an explanation of where and why the rankings disagree.

\section{Approaches to ranking in sports}

Table~\ref{tab:approaches} groups the methods used in sports into five approaches. Approaches 1 and 2 count results without looking at who the opponent was; approaches 3 and 4 take the opponent's strength into account; approach 5 has no direct opponent.

\begin{table}[!htbp]
\centering
\small
\caption{Five approaches to ranking in sports.}
\label{tab:approaches}
\begin{tabularx}{\textwidth}{@{}>{\raggedright\arraybackslash}p{3.1cm}LL@{}}
\toprule
Approach & Main idea & Examples \\
\midrule
1. League table & Points for each result over a season in which every team plays every other team & Premier League, NBA, NHL \\
2. Tournament points & Fixed points by event and round reached, added up over a time window & ATP and WTA rankings; UEFA club coefficients; ISU World Standings \\
3. Elo rating & A rating that moves after every match by the actual minus the expected result & FIDE chess ratings; FIFA men's ranking since 2018 \\
4. Network rating & One calculation over the whole directed network of results & Weighted PageRank; Colley Matrix (college football) \\
5. Judged score & Judges score each performance; the highest total wins & Figure skating; gymnastics \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabularx}
\end{table}

\paragraph{League tables.} When every team plays every other team the same number of times, a points table is a fair ranking. English football adopted three points for a win in 1981, and FIFA adopted the system in 1995 \citep{wiki_3pts}.

\paragraph{Tournament points.} The first ATP rankings, published on 23 August 1973, were based on each player's \emph{average} result. In 1990 they were replaced by a ``best of'' system that adds up a player's best results (14 at first, 18 from 2000, 19 today), and points expire after 52 weeks \citep{wiki_atp}. During the COVID-19 pandemic the ranking was frozen on 16 March 2020 and then calculated over a longer window \citep{atp2020,tennismajors2021}. Under these rules, the points for a win depend on the tournament and the round, not on the opponent.

\paragraph{Elo ratings.} Arpad Elo designed his system for chess \citep{elo1978}. After each match a rating changes by $K(S-E)$, where $S$ is the actual and $E$ the expected result, so a win over a stronger opponent earns more. FIFA has used an Elo-type method for its men's ranking since 16 August 2018 \citep{wiki_fifa}.

\paragraph{Network ratings.} These methods solve one problem over the whole network of results: eigenvector methods \citep{keener1993}, direct and indirect wins \citep{park2005}, the Colley Matrix used in college football \citep{colley2002,wiki_colley}, and weighted PageRank \citep{radicchi2011,govan2008,lazova2015}.

\paragraph{Competition format.} Format matters as much as method. In a round-robin league all teams face the same opponents. In knockout tournaments, as in tennis, a player who loses leaves the event, so over a season each player meets a different set of opponents. When schedules are incomplete, the strength of the opponents differs from player to player, and approaches 3 and 4 have more to add \citep{zhou2020}.

\section{Data}

\paragraph{Tennis.} I use Jeff Sackmann's ATP and WTA data \citep{sackmann,wtadata}. For each season I take all tour-level main-draw matches and remove Davis Cup and Billie Jean King Cup ties, team exhibitions (the Next Gen Finals and the Laver Cup) and walkovers. This gives 164{,}745 men's matches among 4{,}263 players (1973--2025) and 101{,}500 women's matches among 2{,}529 players (1984--2025), plus the first five months of 2026 (1{,}310 and 1{,}264 matches, to 25 May 2026). The 2025 men's season has 2{,}646 matches among 292 players. The official ranking of a season is the last ATP or WTA ranking of the calendar year.

\paragraph{Other sports.} International football: all matches between FIFA members from 16 August 2018, when FIFA adopted its Elo-type method, to November 2026 \citep{intlresults}, with the complete FIFA men's ranking of 18 July 2024 \citep{fifa2024}, the most recent full list I could access. Leagues: the Premier League 2024--25 and 2025--26 \citep{footballdatasets}, the NBA 2024--25 and 2025--26 \citep{hoopr}, the NHL 2024--25 and 2025--26 \citep{fastrhockey}, with the official points standings \citep{statscrew_nhl2425,wiki_nhl2526}, and the NFL 2024 and 2025 regular seasons \citep{nflverse}.

\section{Methods}

\subsection{The network of results}
For a season, each competitor is a node. Every match adds 1 to the weight of the link from the loser to the winner, so if the same player beats the same opponent three times, that link has weight 3. Two players who beat each other are joined by two links, one in each direction, each with its own number of wins (Figure~\ref{fig:h2h}). In leagues with draws, a draw adds a link of weight $0.5$ in each direction.

\begin{figure}[!htbp]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=0.55\textwidth]{figures/top10_head_to_head_2025.png}
\caption{The network of matches among the ATP top 10 of 2025. Each link points to the winner and its label is the number of wins.}
\label{fig:h2h}
\end{figure}

\subsection{Weighted PageRank}
WPR is the long-run behaviour of a random walk on the network. A walker starts at a random player. At each step, with probability $d$ it follows one of the links leaving the current player, that is, it moves from a loser to a player who beat him, choosing each link with probability proportional to its weight. With probability $1-d$ it jumps to a player chosen uniformly at random; it also jumps uniformly when the current player has no losses. The WPR score of a player is the fraction of time the walker spends at that player, so a player scores highly by beating players who score highly.

Let $w_{ji}$ be the weight of the link from $j$ to $i$ (the number of times $i$ beat $j$), $s_j = \sum_i w_{ji}$ the total weight leaving $j$ (its losses), and $N$ the number of players. The WPR scores solve
\begin{equation}
\mathrm{WPR}(i) = \frac{1-d}{N} \;+\; d\sum_{j:\,w_{ji}>0} \mathrm{WPR}(j)\,\frac{w_{ji}}{s_j} \;+\; \frac{d}{N} \sum_{j:\,s_j=0} \mathrm{WPR}(j).
\end{equation}
I use $d = 0.85$, the value of the original PageRank \citep{brin1998}, and compute WPR by power iteration; the results agree with NetworkX \citep{hagberg2008} to within $10^{-11}$.

\subsection{Correlation}
I compare two rankings with Spearman's rank correlation $\rho$ \citep{spearman1904}, which compares the order of the competitors. In tennis I use the top 100 of the official year-end ranking (the players who played at least one tour-level match that season).

\subsection{Predictability}
I measure predictability with the following test. The two rankings at the end of one season, the official ranking and WPR, are used to predict the matches of the next season. For example, if last season's official order is A, B, C, D, E and the PageRank order is B, A, C, E, D, and A and C meet in the new season, both rankings predict that A wins. For each match I write 1 if a ranking predicted the winner correctly and 0 if it did not; the percentage of ones is that ranking's predictability. I use every match of the next season between two players ranked by both rankings: in tennis, 1973 rankings predict 1974 matches, and so on up to 2025 rankings predicting the first months of 2026.

The two rankings make the same prediction for about nine matches in ten, so their percentages are always close. I therefore use three measures, from the simplest to the most decisive (Table~\ref{tab:measures}): the percentage of matches predicted correctly; the percentage correct on the matches where the two rankings disagree, which isolates the real difference between them (binomial test against 50\%); and the number of seasons in which each ranking is ahead, with a paired Wilcoxon signed-rank test, which shows whether a difference holds season after season. Probability scores such as the Brier score or log loss do not apply, because a ranking gives an order, not probabilities.

\subsection{Consistency with the season's own results}
A second, simpler check counts the results of a season that agree with a ranking of the same season: a match is \emph{consistent} if the higher-ranked player won. I compute it for the matches between two players of the official top 10, 20, 50 and 100. Because this test uses the same matches that produced the ranking, I treat it as a description and the next-season test as the fair comparison.

\subsection{Disagreements}
\paragraph{One upset.} To measure what one win is worth, I take the real 2025 network, add one extra win for the ATP number 100 over a single opponent, recompute WPR, and repeat this for every opponent in the ATP top 200. Under ATP rules, the same win is worth the same points whatever the opponent: a first-round win at a Grand Slam is worth $50-10=40$ points \citep{atppoints2024}.

\paragraph{Who gains and who loses.} For the ATP top 100 of every season from 2010 to 2025 (1{,}598 player-seasons) I define a player's \emph{gain} as his position by the ATP ranking minus his position by WPR within the top 100; a positive gain means WPR ranks him higher. I relate the gain to the player's schedule (number of tour-level matches, share of matches at the biggest events, wins over top-20 players) and to the region where he plays.

\subsection{Consistency with the season's own results}

About one result in four is an upset whatever the ranking. For matches between two top-100 players, 67.1\% of men's results agree with the ATP year-end order and 66.4\% with the WPR order; for women, 69.4\% and 68.6\% (Table~\ref{tab:consist}). Among the best players the two are tied: for the men's top 10 and top 20, each ranking is ahead in about half of the seasons, and the official ranking pulls ahead only when the wider field is included. For men, almost all of the gap comes from before 1990 (Figure~\ref{fig:consist}). When the official ranking does not describe the season, as in 2020, WPR agrees with more results.

A higher percentage is not automatically better. I searched for the order of the top 100 that agrees with the most results of a season; it reaches about 76\% for men, but it predicts the next season worse than WPR (62.6\% against 64.0\%, worse in 45 of 53 seasons). An order can be fitted to one season without being a better ranking, which is why the next-season test is the fair comparison.

\subsection{What one upset is worth}

Under ATP rules, one first-round Grand Slam win moves the 2025 number 100, Tristan Schoolkate, up 12 places, whoever the opponent. Under WPR the value depends on the opponent (Figure~\ref{fig:upset} and Table~\ref{tab:upset}): one extra win over Carlos Alcaraz or Jannik Sinner moves him up 64 and 61 places, while a win over a player ranked between 50 and 200 is worth only a few places. For all 21 players ranked 90--110 in 2025, the median WPR rank goes from 104 to 45 after beating Alcaraz, but only to 101 after beating the number 200. Repeating the experiment in every season from 1990 to 2025 (that season's number 100 beats that season's number 1), the gain is always positive (at least 7 places, median 39) and largest when the number one rarely lost.

The mechanism is simple. A win passes on a share of the beaten player's score divided by the number of times he lost. This quantity orders the gains almost perfectly (Spearman 0.96), while the opponent's ATP rank does not (0.44). Beating Alexander Zverev, ATP number 3 but beaten 23 times in 2025, is worth less than beating Novak Djokovic (number 4, beaten 11 times). Losing does not lower the beaten player's own WPR rank in this test (Alcaraz stays first): WPR rewards wins and does not subtract for losses.

Two features of the ATP ranking explain these patterns. It rewards a single deep run heavily: Valentin Vacherot won the 2025 Shanghai Masters as a qualifier, which is worth 1{,}000 ATP points but adds only 11 tour-level wins to the network. And it includes points from the Challenger tour, which is not in the tour-level network, so players with few tour-level matches lose places under WPR. In 2025 the largest losers were Sebasti\'an B\'aez (ATP 45, no wins over top-20 players, 61\% of matches on clay), Valentin Royer and Terence Atmane (21 and 17 tour-level matches); the largest gainers were Benjamin Bonzi (ATP 94) and Roberto Bautista Agut (ATP 90), who each won fewer than half of their tour-level matches but beat three top-20 players.

\paragraph{Location.} South American players are ranked on average 2.0 places lower by WPR than by the ATP ranking (164 player-seasons; all other players $+0.2$; Mann--Whitney $p = 0.014$). Their seasons are clay-heavy (53\% of matches on clay, against 32\% for Europeans), and many of their matches are at ATP 250 events in South America. An ATP 250 title is worth 250 points everywhere, but the fields are not equally strong everywhere (Table~\ref{tab:region}): the median beaten player at ATP 250 events in South America was ranked 89 at the end of the year, against 63 in Asia and 70 in Europe. The same points therefore come with fewer wins over strong players, and WPR gives less credit. Tournament locations were entered by hand, and this is an association, not a proof of cause.

\section{Discussion}

\paragraph{Different rules, similar rankings.} WPR uses only who beat whom in one season; the ATP and WTA rankings use a table of points by tournament and round. The two share no formula, yet they are well correlated in every season except 2020, and in every sport studied. Strong competitors both go deep in big tournaments and beat strong opponents, so both methods see the same signal. This agreement is a validity check, but it cannot show that one ranking is better, because the official ranking is not the truth.

\paragraph{Equal predictability.} The next-season test gives an outside answer. In men's tennis the two rankings predict equally well over five decades, with WPR slightly ahead since the ATP moved to its best-of system; in women's tennis the WTA ranking is slightly but consistently better. In the other sports, with one season each, the differences are small and go both ways. A ranking built only from results is therefore about as good a predictor as the official one, not a better one.

\paragraph{The disagreements are informative.} Where WPR and the official ranking differ, the reason is usually visible: the value of a win over a rarely beaten player, a single deep run, points from lower-tier events, weaker regional fields, NFL teams with easy schedules, and years when the rules changed (2020). These are the places where the official rule rewards something other than beating strong opponents.

\paragraph{Limitations.} Each network covers one calendar season and only tour-level matches, while the ATP ranking also counts Challenger results and only a player's best results. The damping factor is chosen, not learned. The team sports and international football cover one or two seasons each, and the FIFA ranking is from July 2024. Several facts in Section~2 are taken from Wikipedia and should be replaced by official rulebooks. Where competitors meet very few opponents, one upset can move a large share of the score \citep{zhou2020}; in mixed martial arts, for example, fighters meet only a tiny fraction of the possible opponents, and the method becomes unstable.

%======================================================================
\section{Future work}

\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{More seasons in other sports}, so that the predictability test has as many seasons as in tennis.
  \item \textbf{Time.} Let older matches count less, so that the network can span more than one season and be compared with the 52-week official ranking \citep{motegi2012}.
  \item \textbf{Learned weights.} Learn the weight of each match (tournament, round, surface, score) from data instead of choosing it.
  \item \textbf{Sports without head-to-head matches.} Turn each golf or figure-skating event into pairwise results (each player beats everyone who finished behind him), so that WPR can be compared with the Official World Golf Ranking and the ISU World Standings.
\end{itemize}

%======================================================================
\section{Conclusion}

A ranking built only from who beat whom, with weighted PageRank, is well correlated with the official rankings in tennis and in five other competitions, and it predicts the next season's matches about as well as the official ranking. Its value is in the disagreements: they show what each official ranking rule rewards, such as deep runs at the biggest events, the number of tournaments entered and the region where a player competes, and they give a simple, transparent check on any official ranking.
